# CAPABLE programming evidence

## Question and scope

This note evaluates constraints for a self-paced, 12-week program with three
formal sessions per training cycle: Lower, Upper, and Conditioning. Each
four-week phase repeats one template of each type. The intended outcomes are
general strength, meaningful heavy loading, flexibility and active end-range
strength, long-term physical capacity, and progress toward a handstand, bar
muscle-up, and front lever.

The evidence is strongest for resistance training, general flexibility, and
concurrent training. Direct dose-response research for adult recreational
handstands, bar muscle-ups, front levers, and the pancake is sparse. Where the
evidence does not establish an exact prescription, this note labels the design
choice as a **programming inference** rather than presenting it as fact.

## Bottom line

Nine stable workout templates can work, but only if the labels are treated as
**emphases**, not isolated body-part days:

- One weekly Lower and one weekly Upper exposure can produce some strength
  gains, particularly in less-trained people, but current guidance favors
  training every major muscle group at least twice weekly for strength and
  health. The Conditioning session should therefore contain a modest second
  whole-body resistance exposure (for example carries, a squat or hinge, and a
  push/pull), not cardio alone.
- Three advanced gymnastics goals do not fit as equal priorities into one
  weekly skill block. Handstand can receive a short exposure every Upper day;
  muscle-up and front-lever work need a primary/secondary hierarchy, with their
  prerequisite strength doing double duty. Once-weekly direct practice should
  be expected to progress slowly. Short, optional, low-fatigue practice exposures
  are the cleanest way to increase skill frequency without adding another full
  workout.
- A beginner pancake needs at least two weekly exposures. Combine controlled
  loaded range with relaxed static range rather than expecting a single Lower
  session to change it.
- A separate Conditioning day with recovery between sessions makes concurrent
  training compatible with the strength goal. High-skill and heavy work should
  precede fatigue.
- Repeating exercises for four weeks supports specificity and measurement.
  Simple performance-based progression is evidence-aligned; a mandatory
  fourth-week deload is not. Week 4 should consolidate and assess, reducing
  volume only when fatigue or technique calls for it.

## 1. Strength frequency, heavy loading, and bone health

### Evidence

The 2026 American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) position stand synthesized
137 reviews and reports that voluntary strength is enhanced by heavier loads
(at least 80% 1RM), full range of motion, 2–3 sets, placing priority exercises
early, and training at least twice per week. It also reports that training to
momentary failure and complex periodization do not consistently improve
outcomes for healthy adults
([Currier et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41843416/)). The official
ACSM summary says to train all major muscle groups at least two days per week
([ACSM infographic, 2026](https://www.acsm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Position-Stand-infographic.pdf)).

A small 10-week study in 30 untrained young men found significant elbow-flexor
strength and size gains with equal weekly volume performed once or twice per
week, without a statistically significant difference between groups
([Gentil et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24732784/)). This is
evidence that one weekly exposure can work, not evidence that it is generally
optimal: the sample was small, male, untrained, and the outcome was a single
muscle group measured by isokinetic peak torque.

For adult bone health, ACSM recommends moderate-to-high bone-loading forces,
resistance exercise 2–3 times per week, and weight-bearing/impact activity 3–5
times per week
([Kohrt et al., 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15514517/)). More broadly,
WHO recommends moderate-or-higher muscle strengthening for all major muscle
groups on at least two days per week and 150–300 minutes of moderate aerobic
activity or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity across the week
([WHO, 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK566046/)).

### Programming inference for CAPABLE

- A dedicated Lower day and Upper day are acceptable **anchors**, but one
  exposure per region is a minimum-efficiency compromise, not an optimal
  strength prescription.
- Make Conditioning a second, lower-volume resistance contact for the whole
  body: one locomotion/impact option where appropriate, one loaded carry, one
  lower-body pattern, and one push or pull. This helps the program satisfy the
  two-day major-muscle requirement without turning it into a fourth workout.
- Use one priority barbell lift on Lower and one or two priority upper-body
  lifts on Upper. A novice should first earn consistent technique with moderate
  loads; later phases can expose selected main-lift sets to roughly 75–85% 1RM
  or RPE 7–8. “Heavy” should not mean maximal or grinding.
- Bone-health language is justified only if the whole program includes
  repeated resistance plus weight-bearing loading. Cycling or rowing alone is
  useful conditioning but does not replace impact/weight-bearing work for the
  skeletal goal.
- One weekly conditioning workout does not, by itself, meet public-health
  aerobic guidance. CAPABLE should explicitly pair the program with regular
  walking, cycling, or other easy activity outside the three formal sessions.

## 2. Fitting handstand, bar muscle-up, and front lever into one Upper day

### Evidence

Direct trials do not establish an optimal weekly frequency for these three
skills in recreational adults. General motor-learning evidence cannot be
translated into a precise gymnastics dose, but a randomized laboratory study
found better acquisition from spaced than massed practice for a sequential
motor task
([Song, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4395711/)). This supports
the broad idea of multiple short, fresh exposures, while its simple task and
short time frame limit direct application to gymnastics.

World Gymnastics' long-term development guidance emphasizes gradual loading,
basic structures, flexibility and power before concentrating on difficult
elements
([World Gymnastics Age Group Programme](https://www.worldgymnastics.sport/site/pages/education-agegroup.php)).
Its men's technical manual presents developmental structures and progressions,
including handstand, hanging/swing work, and front-lever elements, rather than
treating advanced elements as entry-level exercises
([FIG Men's Artistic Gymnastics manual](https://www.gymnastics.sport/site/pages/education/agegroup-mag-manual-e.pdf)).

### Programming inference for CAPABLE

An Upper session should use a hierarchy rather than three equal skill blocks:

1. **Handstand, 6–10 minutes:** first, while wrists, shoulders, and attention
   are fresh. Regress from freestanding balance to chest-to-wall or box-pike
   work as needed.
2. **Primary pulling skill, 8–12 minutes:** either muscle-up or front lever,
   chosen by phase and readiness.
3. **Secondary pulling skill, 4–6 minutes:** low-volume prerequisite work for
   the other goal, stopped well before form failure.
4. **Strength work:** vertical pull, press/dip, row, straight-arm scapular work,
   and trunk stiffness should reinforce the same prerequisites rather than add
   unrelated volume.

This fits all three goals into one day, but it does not make them equal
priorities. Four direct exposures in a phase are unlikely to produce rapid
skill acquisition. The program should promise progress in prerequisites and
progression level, not completion of a muscle-up or front lever in 12 weeks.

If faster skill learning matters, add optional 5–10-minute practice doses when
fresh—especially wall handstand line/balance work—and record them as practice,
not as full workouts. Another in-program option is a short handstand line
exposure before the Conditioning session, before fatigue begins.

Exact prerequisites such as a required number of pull-ups or a specific hold
time are coaching conventions, not validated universal thresholds. CAPABLE
should gate progression by pain-free control, repeatable positions, and clean
reps rather than publish unsupported guarantees.

## 3. Flexibility and end-range strength

### Evidence

ACSM recommends flexibility work for the major muscle-tendon groups on at least
two days per week, accumulating about 60 seconds per exercise
([Garber et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21694556/)). In untrained
adults, five weeks of full-range resistance training improved flexibility
similarly to static stretching while also improving strength
([Morton et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21969080/)). A newer,
small randomized trial likewise found that full-ROM resistance training and
static stretching could improve hip/lower-back-extensor flexibility, although
only 18 people were randomized and group sizes were very small
([Rosenfeldt et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11212372/)).

These studies support training through controllable range, but they do not
validate a particular “pancake protocol” or prove that loaded stretching alone
is superior to other methods.

### Programming inference for CAPABLE

- Expose the pancake pattern at least twice per cycle: a meaningful active,
  loaded dose on Lower and a lighter active/relaxed dose on Conditioning.
- Use both sides of the adaptation: slow strength through available range
  (supported Cossack squats, elevated straddle good mornings, controlled
  hinges, and straddle lift/compression work) plus relaxed static range after
  training.
- For static work, accumulate roughly 60 seconds for the chosen position using
  tolerable bouts. For active end-range work, 2–3 technically clean sets is a
  conservative starting inference, not an evidence-established pancake dose.
- Start a very stiff trainee on enough elevation, knee bend, or hand support to
  keep the pelvis and spine controllable. Progress one variable at a time:
  range/support, external load, reps, or hold time.
- “Decompression” is a useful UI/session label for low-intensity breathing and
  relaxed range; it should not be described as a special recovery mechanism.
  Sharp pain, tingling, or nerve-like symptoms are stop signals, not targets to
  stretch through.

## 4. Strength and conditioning together

### Evidence

Concurrent endurance and resistance work is not automatically incompatible
with strength. In a nine-week randomized study of 29 moderately active men,
resistance-only training and same-day concurrent training (HIIT three hours
before or after resistance work) produced comparable lower-body maximal
strength and lean-mass gains; exercise order affected some power outcomes
([Lee et al., 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0233134)).
The study is reassuring for a modest hybrid program, but its small, all-male
sample and specific three-day protocol do not settle every mode, dose, or
ordering question.

### Programming inference for CAPABLE

- Lower / Upper / Conditioning with a recovery day between sessions is a sound
  default. In a self-paced program, recommend at least one non-training day
  between formal sessions and discourage doubling up to “catch up.”
- Put coordination-heavy gymnastics, jumping/power, and heavy strength before
  metabolically fatiguing work. Do not place muscle-up or front-lever attempts
  inside a conditioning circuit.
- Keep most conditioning repeatable (roughly RPE 6–7) and use hard intervals
  selectively. Prefer lower-muscle-damage modes such as bike, rower, sled, or
  carries when recovery for the next Lower session is the constraint.
- If strength and conditioning must share a session, perform the phase's
  priority first. The exact order matters less than preserving quality and
  managing total fatigue in this general-trainee context.

## 5. Four-week repetition, progression, and consolidation

### Evidence

ACSM's progression model recommends a 2–10% load increase when a trainee can
perform one or two repetitions beyond the target at the current load
([ACSM, 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11828249/)). The newer ACSM review
finds that complex periodization does not consistently improve outcomes for
the average healthy adult
([Currier et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41843416/)).

Evidence for a mandatory deload every fourth week is limited. In one randomized
trial of resistance-trained adults, completely abstaining from resistance
training for the fifth week of a nine-week high-volume program produced worse
lower-body strength outcomes than continuous training, while hypertrophy,
power, and local endurance were similar
([Coleman et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10809978/)).
That trial tested a week off, not a modest volume reduction, so it neither
proves nor disproves a lighter consolidation week.

### Programming inference for CAPABLE

Keeping the exercises stable for four exposures is useful for learning,
specificity, and trustworthy records. Progress by earned performance:

- **Week 1 — establish:** select regressions, loads, range, and pacing that
  produce clean work around RPE 6–7.
- **Week 2 — progress:** improve one variable while keeping the exercise and
  quality standard stable.
- **Week 3 — build:** use the highest productive load/difficulty of the phase,
  generally no higher than RPE 8.
- **Week 4 — consolidate:** repeat clean work and record a submaximal benchmark.
  Reduce a set or 10–25% of volume only when soreness, declining performance,
  joint tolerance, or technique warrants it; do not prescribe a week off by
  default.

Because the program is self-paced, “week” should mean the next completed
Lower–Upper–Conditioning cycle, not a calendar deadline. After a long break,
repeat or regress the last successful prescription rather than skipping ahead.

## 6. Novice-to-intermediate safety and exercise selection

### Evidence

ACSM preparticipation guidance bases the need for medical clearance on current
activity, known cardiovascular/metabolic/renal disease, symptoms, and intended
intensity. Active, asymptomatic people without known disease can generally
progress gradually; new relevant symptoms are a reason to stop and seek
clearance
([Riebe et al., 2015](https://journals.lww.com/acsm-msse/fulltext/2015/11000/updating_acsm_s_recommendations_for_exercise.28.aspx)).
World Gymnastics similarly emphasizes gradual loading, foundational physical
preparation, balanced development, and recovery rather than early pursuit of
difficult elements
([World Gymnastics](https://www.worldgymnastics.sport/site/pages/education-agegroup.php)).

### Programming inference for CAPABLE

- Give every advanced movement an explicit regression ladder. A trainee should
  never have to attempt the named end skill to complete the workout.
- Use quality-based stopping rules: end a gymnastics set when line, scapular
  control, grip, landing, or confidence deteriorates. Avoid routine failure on
  both skills and heavy lifts.
- Progress handstand loading gradually because the wrist becomes a
  weight-bearing joint. Progress muscle-up and front-lever work conservatively
  because both accumulate substantial shoulder, elbow, and grip stress.
- Keep dynamic/kipping muscle-up attempts out of unsupervised beginner work.
  Use bar support, pulling strength, controlled swing shapes, and assisted
  transition work first; coaching or a spotter is appropriate for novel dynamic
  attempts.
- Scale impact to training history, body mass, symptoms, and landing skill.
  Carries and resistance training can remain when high impact is inappropriate.
- This is a general program, not rehabilitation. Persistent pain, neurological
  symptoms, significant previous injury, pregnancy-related considerations, or
  known medical disease require individualized professional advice.

## Review criteria for the actual nine workouts

The program should not be approved merely because it contains familiar
exercise names. Across the nine templates, verify that:

1. Every major movement pattern receives at least two weekly contacts when the
   Conditioning session is counted.
2. Each phase has one clear strength adaptation and one clear flexibility
   adaptation, with exercises that remain stable for four exposures.
3. Gymnastics work is progression-gated, early in the session, and organized as
   handstand plus primary/secondary pulling skill—not three maximal blocks.
4. Pancake-related range appears at least twice per cycle and records both a
   range/support measure and an active-control measure.
5. Conditioning is measurable and repeatable, mostly submaximal, and does not
   hide high-skill gymnastics under fatigue.
6. Week-to-week progression changes one meaningful variable and Week 4 is an
   assessment/consolidation exposure rather than an automatic rest week.
7. Every session has a useful record field that can drive the next exposure:
   load and RPE, progression/regression, clean hold/reps, range/support, or
   pace/work completed.

## Evidence gaps to preserve in product language

- No high-quality evidence establishes that this exact three-day split is
  optimal.
- No trial found here validates a precise weekly dose or 12-week success rate
  for adult handstand, bar muscle-up, front lever, or pancake training.
- “Longevity” is an umbrella outcome. The program can support recognized
  components of healthy aging—strength, aerobic capacity, balance, range of
  motion, and bone-loading activity—but should not promise lifespan or injury
  prevention.
- Individual response, prior training, sleep, nutrition, age, sex, injury
  history, and actual time between self-paced sessions will materially change
  progress.

## 7. Strength calibration: Push Jerk, e1RM, and a CAPABLE prescription

### What Push Jerk currently publishes

The publicly indexed Push Jerk programming reviewed on August 11, 2026 uses
percentages in two related ways:

- Its mid-2026 18-week “5/3/1 EMOM” cycle prescribes percentages of **1RM**
  directly. Examples include back squat sets of 5 at 65%, 75%, and 85%
  ([July 13, 2026](https://pushjerk.com/mon-jul-13-2026/)); front and back squat
  sets of 5/3/1 at 70%, 80%, and 90%
  ([June 29, 2026](https://pushjerk.com/mon-jun-29-2026/)); and ascending
  5/4/3/2/1 squat work at 75%, 80%, 85%, 90%, and 95%
  ([July 20, 2026, on the program index](https://pushjerk.com/)).
- Its 16-week Inverted Juggernaut cycle, still published in early 2026, defines
  **T1RM** as 90% of a true or estimated 1RM. It uses four-week waves, updates
  T1RM from the Week 3 AMRAP, and calls Week 4 a deload
  ([cycle description, December 29, 2025](https://pushjerk.com/mon-dec-29-2025/)).
  A January example prescribes high-volume work at 60% T1RM
  ([January 5, 2026](https://pushjerk.com/mon-jan-5-2026/)), while its deload
  uses 40%, 50%, and 60% T1RM
  ([January 19, 2026](https://pushjerk.com/mon-jan-19-2026/)).

Push Jerk also provides a current
[Rep Max Calculator](https://pushjerk.com/repmax/). Its published page source
calculates 1RM as `weight / (1.0278 - 0.0278 × reps)`, the Brzycki equation,
then rounds down to a multiple of five. The visible page does not name the
equation or state a maximum valid rep count. The Push Jerk pages reviewed refer
to a “true, or estimated, 1RM” and provide calculators, but do **not** provide a
complete estimation protocol covering warm-up, acceptable rep range,
proximity to failure, technique validity, or what to do after a poor-readiness
day. Its 2024 test week instead included progressively heavier singles through
100%+ of known 1RM
([May 22, 2024](https://pushjerk.com/wed-may-22-2024/)).

Those are observations about Push Jerk, not a recommendation to copy its
volume, AMRAP-to-failure work, or mandatory deload into CAPABLE.

### Evidence relevant to a submaximal estimate

Prediction equations are estimates, not interchangeable measurements of a
true maximum. Brzycki originally presented the equation as a practical way to
predict a one-repetition maximum from repetitions to fatigue
([Brzycki, 1993](https://doi.org/10.1080/07303084.1993.10606684)). In a study
of 220 men with varied training backgrounds, the
Brzycki equation was the most accurate of six equations for bench-press sets
of ten or fewer repetitions to failure, but errors and equation-dependent bias
remained
([Mayhew et al., 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7500624/)). In 70 adults
aged 18–69, 5RM performance predicted measured bench-press and leg-press 1RM
more accurately than 10RM or 20RM performance
([Reynolds et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16937972/)). These
findings favor a low-to-moderate rep estimate and argue against deriving a
strength max from high-repetition conditioning work. Comparisons across bench
press, squat, and deadlift also show equation- and exercise-specific error
([LeSuer et al., 1997](https://doi.org/10.1519/00124278-199711000-00001)).

The repetitions-in-reserve (RIR) RPE scale has empirical support, but skill in
using it matters. Its original validation compared experienced and novice
squatters
([Zourdos et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26049792/)). In a
separate study, people tended to underestimate how many repetitions they could
perform before failure, while accuracy improved with resistance-training
experience
([Steele et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.4105)). Practical guidance
from the RIR scale's authors specifically cautions that novices should practice
recording RIR rather than base progression on it alone
([Helms et al., 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4961270/)). This
supports combining a percentage anchor with an RPE ceiling instead of treating
either number as infallible.

Finally, ACSM's current position stand reports that heavier loading enhances
strength, while training to momentary failure does not consistently improve
outcomes
([Currier et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41843416/)). A
longevity-focused program therefore does not need true-max or failure testing
to prescribe useful strength work.

### Exact CAPABLE recommendation (programming inference)

The following is a conservative implementation choice assembled from those
findings. The exact combination has not itself been tested as a protocol.

#### Establish the estimate

Use a technically clean set of **4–6 repetitions at RPE 9** (approximately one
repetition in reserve), performed after a specific warm-up and before other
fatiguing work. Use safeties or a spotter where appropriate. Stop for pain,
loss of range, or a material technique change; do not turn the set into a true
rep max.

Calculate:

```text
effective reps = completed reps + estimated RIR
e1RM = load / (1.0278 - 0.0278 × effective reps)
training max (TM) = 90% × e1RM, rounded down to a loadable increment
```

For example, `100 kg × 5 @ RPE 9` is treated as approximately six effective
repetitions: e1RM ≈ 116 kg and TM ≈ 104 kg before rounding down.

Adding estimated RIR to completed reps adapts a reps-to-failure equation for a
non-failure set. That is a **programming inference**, not a separately
validated Brzycki protocol. Limit its use to 4–6 completed reps, 1–3 RIR, and
no more than eight effective reps. The 90% training max and the RPE caps below are
deliberate error buffers. For a new lifter who cannot yet judge RIR, begin from
a deliberately light technical estimate, use completed repetitions without
adding RIR, and calibrate only after several weeks of logging RPE. This
intentionally underestimates capacity.

Use this method for the same standardized main lift—same equipment, range, and
execution. Do not transfer a barbell e1RM across variations. Do not calculate a
weighted pull-up or dip from external load alone: body mass is part of the
system load and makes the estimate less portable, so CAPABLE should use RPE and
rep progression for those movements unless it implements the accounting
explicitly.

#### Prescribe one four-week exposure

Percentage chooses the starting load; the RPE cap always has veto power.

| Week | Main-lift prescription | RPE cap | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 3 × 5 at 80% TM | 7 | Establish repeatable volume |
| 2 | 3 × 4 at 85% TM | 7.5 | Add useful work without grinding |
| 3 | 1 calibration set of 4–6 at RPE 9, beginning around 90–92.5% TM; then 2 × 3 at 85% TM | 9 on calibration, 7.5 on back-offs | Heavy exposure and a non-failure estimate |
| 4 | 2 × 4 at 80% TM | 7 | Consolidate technique with lower volume |

Because TM is 90% of e1RM, 80%, 85%, 90%, and 92.5% TM are approximately 72%,
76.5%, 81%, and 83.25% of e1RM before rounding. This introduces genuinely
heavy work in Week 3 without making every week heavy. It is appropriate for
the primary barbell lift, not for gymnastics skill practice.

If the first work set exceeds its cap or loses the prescribed range, reduce
the remaining load by 2.5–5%. If it still exceeds the cap, omit a set. Never
increase load merely to hit the percentage, and never use a failed or assisted
rep to estimate strength.

#### Update the training max

Recalculate from the Week 3 calibration only when all of the following are
true:

- 4–6 clean repetitions were completed at RPE 8.5–9;
- range, equipment, and technique match the original estimate;
- the set occurred before conditioning and was pain-free; and
- Weeks 2 and 3 were completed without repeatedly breaking their RPE caps.

Require two qualifying estimates for the same lift and setup, reasonably
consistent within 5%; use the lower estimate rather than the single highest.
If that conservative e1RM is at least **2.5% higher**, set the next phase's TM
to 90% of it. Cap a phase-to-phase increase at **2.5% for an upper-body lift**
and **5% for a lower-body lift**. Otherwise keep TM unchanged. Reduce TM by
5–10% when two consecutive exposures exceed their RPE caps despite a session
load reduction, two valid estimates show a meaningful decline, or a layoff has
materially changed readiness. These thresholds are conservative programming
rules, not physiological cut-points established by trials.

Do not carry an estimate from one lift variation to another. If the next phase
changes the main lift, establish a separate conservative TM rather than calling
the new exercise an update of the old one. After more than 14 days without the
lift, repeat the last successful load or reduce it by roughly 5%; after more
than 28 days, restart with a Week 1-style exposure before recalibrating. These
time thresholds are also programming inferences.

The application should store the source set (load, reps, RPE/RIR, exercise
variation, and date) alongside e1RM and TM. That makes the percentage auditable
and lets a user distinguish a calculated estimate from a tested maximum.
