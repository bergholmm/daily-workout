# MILE to The Next Level — official-source review

Date: 14 August 2026

## Question and method

This note examines how Steph Rose presents Make It Look Easy (MILE) and The
Next Level (TNL). It compares their identity, audience, structure,
progression, equipment, mobility, strength, and bodyweight work.

The main evidence comes from two current first-party sales pages:

- [Make It Look Easy](https://steph.mvt.so/programs)
- [The Next Level](https://steph.mvt.so/the-next-level)

The local database contains a user-entered transcription of the nine MILE
workouts. It is used only to examine session structure and movement threads.
It is a secondary source because this report did not independently compare
every stored exercise with the current paid app.

Official claims and programming inferences are separated below.

## Main finding

MILE and TNL share a training philosophy, but they are different products.

- MILE is a guided 12-week course. It teaches the method and moves through
  three four-week levels.
- TNL is an ongoing workout library. It adds one new workout each month and
  lets the member select workouts in any order.
- Steph positions TNL after MILE for people who know the basic movements and
  want more challenge and variety.
- TNL is therefore the next product after MILE. The official page does not
  present it as a second fixed 12-week curriculum.

These statements come directly from the official
[MILE](https://steph.mvt.so/programs) and
[TNL](https://steph.mvt.so/the-next-level) pages.

## What MILE means in the official material

### Identity and training idea

The MILE page describes the problem as a lack of strength and control in
restricted ranges, not simply a lack of stretching. Its promised results are
stronger deep positions, better mobility, improved athletic ability, and more
durable joints. It presents strength and mobility as one training problem.
This is different from a standard strength plan with a short mobility block
added at the end. [MILE official page](https://steph.mvt.so/programs)

### Three-level progression

The official page gives each four-week level a clear purpose:

1. Level 1 lays the foundation.
2. Level 2 builds on the first level and introduces more advanced
   progressions.
3. Level 3 uses more advanced strength and mobility drills and asks the user
   to control each repetition.

This is a movement-difficulty progression. The page does not publish a load
percentage system, formal testing week, or exact pass standard between levels.
[MILE official page](https://steph.mvt.so/programs)

### Audience and instruction

The page markets MILE from beginner to advanced, but its main product role is
instruction. It is for a person who wants a clear sequence, detailed movement
tutorials, and an explanation of how Steph builds complete workouts.
[MILE official page](https://steph.mvt.so/programs)

## What TNL means in the official material

### Position after MILE

The TNL page says that most people start with MILE and then move to TNL. It
also tells unsure users to start with MILE and move to TNL when they are ready
for more. Readiness is described as knowing the basics and wanting more
challenge and variety. [TNL official page](https://steph.mvt.so/the-next-level)

### Library, not course

TNL adds one workout each month to a growing library. A member can select a
workout and schedule it on a personal calendar. The library can be used with
MILE, a sport, martial arts, running, or another gym plan. It also supports
movement-level result logging and an app-led workout mode with sets and rest
periods. [TNL official page](https://steph.mvt.so/the-next-level)

### Range and variety

The stated categories include upper body, lower body, full body, kettlebell,
bodyweight, active recovery, landmine, loaded mobility, and core. The stated
outcomes include deeper-range strength, athletic movement, conditioning, body
control, durable joints, and confidence with new movements.
[TNL official page](https://steph.mvt.so/the-next-level)

## Session structure in the local MILE transcription

The local database has nine MILE workout definitions: Lower, Upper, and
Conditioning for each four-week level. This supports a simple three-workout
phase model.

The Lower and Upper workouts generally use this session grammar:

1. Movement preparation
2. Three paired main-work blocks
3. Decompression

The Conditioning workouts generally use three circuits and decompression.
Most work uses three sets. The workouts use a small group of tools and usually
keep related tools together inside a block.

This section comes from the local `programs` and `program_workouts` tables,
queried on 14 August 2026.

## Facts the public pages do not establish

The official pages do not provide the following information:

- A fixed TNL sequence from one workout to the next
- A TNL 12-week progression
- A defined handstand progression
- A defined handstand push-up progression
- A defined pull-up or muscle-up progression
- A stated rule for weekly training frequency
- A published readiness test for moving from MILE to TNL
- A promise that any named advanced skill will be achieved

The local MILE transcription reaches a chin-up and an HSPU variation in Level
3. It does not show a muscle-up thread or a dedicated handstand skill thread.
That supports the user's view that the upper-body path can be extended, but it
does not prove that the official TNL library contains the desired skill
progressions.

## Programming inferences for the new product

The following items are design conclusions. They are not official claims from
Steph Rose.

### 1. Build a structured sequel, not a copy of official TNL

The clearest product direction is a **structured next-level program**:

- Keep MILE's guided 12-week and three-level structure.
- Keep its three-workout phase model.
- Keep its movement preparation, paired work, simple tool use, and
  decompression.
- Raise the upper-body progression toward pull-up, explosive pull,
  muscle-up transition, handstand, and HSPU work.
- Keep lower-body range, loaded mobility, ground movement, carries, and
  conditioning as equal parts of the identity.

This combines MILE's teaching and progression with TNL's stated
intermediate-to-advanced direction. It would not reproduce TNL's official
library format.

### 2. Do not turn it into a conventional strength split

MILE's stored sessions progress movement problems. They do not center the
program on a barbell squat, bench press, or deadlift. Loaded resistance helps
the user own a position, produce force, carry load, or connect movements.

A sequel should use the same rule: select external load when it improves the
target movement. Do not make a conventional heavy lift the identity unless
that is a separate product decision.

### 3. Advance the upper-body threads without removing the MILE balance

The stored MILE upper-body sequence is simple: vertical pull plus push,
horizontal pull plus loaded press, then carry or shoulder-control work. A
next-level version can keep these roles and raise the difficulty:

- Vertical pull can progress from strict or weighted pull-up to high pull and
  assisted muscle-up transition.
- Push can progress from handstand position to partial-range and full-range
  HSPU work.
- Horizontal pull should remain for balanced pulling volume.
- Loaded press, carry, hanging, and shoulder range should remain for durable
  positions.

The advanced skill must have a fallback at every level. Phase completion must
not require a complete muscle-up or freestanding HSPU.

### 4. Keep the library idea separate from the program idea

The user now needs to choose between two valid products:

- A fixed 12-week sequel to MILE, with defined progression and outcomes
- An ongoing TNL-style library, with independent workouts and monthly variety

Trying to make one data model serve both without a clear priority will make
the training structure unclear again.

## Recommended next grilling question

The next question should be:

> Are we building a fixed 12-week sequel to MILE, or an ongoing workout
> library like the official TNL product?

If the answer is a fixed sequel, the working identity can be:

> A 12-week intermediate movement program that keeps MILE's simple full-body
> method and adds clear pull-up, muscle-up, handstand, and HSPU progression.

That identity is specific enough to guide the next interview. It also keeps
longevity, mobility, loaded range, and simple equipment as permanent standards
instead of treating them as optional accessories.
